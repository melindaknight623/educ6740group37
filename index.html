DLO 2: What It Means To Have Dyslexia (And What It Doesn't)

There are a number of common misunderstandings and misconceptions about what it mean to have dyslexia. Click on each dropdown to reveal what is true or false about dyslexia.

People with dyslexia may have difficulties while reading, because words and letters may appear 'jumbled' or 'flipped'.
False - Dyslexia is a neurological condition which is mainly linked to issues with phonological decoding and awareness, rather than the way words or letters visually present.

For people with dyslexia, which factor mainly influences their ability to understand unfamiliar words? A) Phonological decoding or B) Visual confusion while reading
A) Phonological decoding - It is widely assumed that individuals with dyslexia have difficulties solely with visual confusion of words, this is not the case.

Targeted strategies, lesson adaptability and one-on-one guidance are all key components to assisting students with dyslexia.
True - Dyslexia is a lifelong condition which effects up to 1 in 5 people globally, and 10% of the Australian population. Early identification of the disability and providing immediate assistance, is key to a student's learning process.

Students who are diagnosed with dyslexia cannot understand texts and their meaning.
False - Often students with dyslexia have a higher innate ability to understand texts and books through listening comprehension, that is, when they are being read to.

Students with dyslexia struggle with phonological awareness, due to their lower intelligence.
False - Dyslexia is a neurological condition which impacts an individual's ability to decode written language, which has no link to lower intelligence levels amongst those with the condition.

Orthographic mapping and orthographic coding can be an issue for individuals with dyslexia.
True - Individuals with dyslexia will not be able to decode written words effectively. Therefore, they may not recognise word patterns or structures, which inevitably affects their ability to recall words from memory.

Dyslexia occurs more frequently in lower socioeconomic areas.
False - Dyslexia occurs in up to 1 in 5 people globally, and 10% of the Australian population, regardless of social and cultural factors. Although, these factors could influence the number of cases which are accurately recognised and recorded.

Dyslexia can cause issues and barriers in other areas of education.
True - All subjects that require reading and writing will need appropriate supports and adjustments, so that they are accessible for students with dyslexia. For example, word problems and equations in mathematics.

Main Takeaways From This Exerpt To Improve Our Teaching

Praise

  • Praise can be a rare occurrence for students with disabilities as traditional systems typically reward standard academic outputs (Gibby-Leversuch et al., 2021).
  • It is important to be targeted and praise specific behaviours (Gable et al., 2009). This can help to build self-esteem in students and break the cycle of demotivation that can occur.

Creativity

  • Similar to Jackie’s story in The Inclusive Teacher (Armstrong & Roy, 2026, p29), where she excels artistically, Dan Sultan has also excelled artistically. This isn’t uncommon, a meta-analysis found that individuals with dyslexia often show distinct advantages when isolating specific facets of divergent thinking, such as originality and flexibility in visual-spatial or non-verbal tasks (Gutiérrez-Ortega et al., 2023)
  • A dyslexic student’s hobbies and interests can be opportunity to engage them and help them overcome anxiety and foster literacy and learning progress. In this case and that of Jackie, targeting activities associated with songwriting and reading about other performers (Armstrong & Roy, 2026).
